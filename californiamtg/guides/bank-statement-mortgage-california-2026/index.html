Bank Statement Mortgage California: What the Lender Is Actually Looking At

A bank statement loan is not simply “add up the deposits.” The useful work is separating eligible business income from transfers, one-time items and expenses so the lender sees a file that makes sense.

Reviewed August 20, 2026 · Anatoliy Kanevsky, NMLS #2775380 · CA Broker DRE #01385024

Quick answer

A bank statement mortgage is an alternative-documentation program commonly used by self-employed borrowers whose tax returns do not fully reflect current business cash flow. Depending on the lender, the analysis may use personal statements, business statements, or both. Many programs review a 12- or 24-month history, but the exact qualifying formula is lender-specific.

Who usually looks at a bank statement mortgage

  • Business owners with substantial legitimate tax deductions.
  • Self-employed borrowers whose current deposits are stronger than taxable income shown on returns.
  • Independent contractors and professionals with irregular monthly income.
  • Borrowers with multiple businesses or mixed income sources that do not fit cleanly into a W-2 file.

Personal statements vs. business statements

There is no universal answer that one is better. A personal-statement program may work well when business income is regularly transferred to a personal account in a consistent, traceable way. A business-statement program may work better when the company’s operating deposits show the real cash-flow pattern more clearly.

With business statements, a lender may apply an expense factor or require support for the business’s operating expenses. That is why two lenders can look at the same gross deposits and calculate different qualifying income.

What usually gets removed or questioned

The lender is trying to identify recurring business revenue, not every dollar that happened to enter an account. Items that commonly need explanation include:

  • Transfers between your own accounts.
  • Loan proceeds or capital contributions.
  • One-time asset sales.
  • Refunds, reversals and duplicate deposits.
  • Large deposits that do not match the normal business pattern.
  • Personal funds moving through a business account.

The 12-month vs. 24-month question

Many programs are built around either 12 months or 24 months of statements. A shorter period can better reflect a business that has recently improved, while a longer history may give the lender more confidence in the stability of income. The best choice depends on the deposit trend and the lender’s rules — not simply on which option sounds easier.

Why the expense factor matters

Gross business deposits are not the same as personal income. A business has costs. Some lenders use a standard expense factor; others may permit a different calculation when supported by acceptable documentation. This is one of the most important places to compare programs because the same business can produce materially different qualifying income under different lender methodologies.

What we organize before asking a lender for an answer

  • Which account or combination of accounts best represents recurring income.
  • How much of the deposit history is true business revenue.
  • Whether transfers can be identified and excluded cleanly.
  • Whether the business is trending up, down, or flat.
  • Entity type, ownership percentage and time in business.
  • Purchase price or property value, loan amount, occupancy and down payment/equity.
  • Credit profile, reserves and any other income that should be considered.

Bank statement does not always mean the best answer

If tax returns support the loan, conventional, high-balance or jumbo full-documentation financing may be more efficient. If the property is an investment, DSCR may be simpler because it focuses on rental cash flow instead of the borrower’s business income. If 1099 income is clean and consistent, a 1099-based program may be worth comparing.

That is why the right question is not “Can I get a bank statement loan?” It is “Which qualifying method produces the strongest eligible structure for my deal?”

Before you send statements: a simple file-prep check

  • Use complete statement PDFs rather than screenshots.
  • Do not remove pages, even if a page looks blank.
  • Identify recurring transfers between your own accounts.
  • Be ready to explain unusually large deposits.
  • Separate business revenue from borrowed funds or one-time transactions.
  • Know which business entity receives the income and your ownership percentage.
