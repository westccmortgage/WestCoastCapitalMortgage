Asset Depletion Mortgage California: When the Balance Sheet Is Stronger Than the Paycheck

A borrower can have substantial cash, brokerage and retirement assets while showing little conventional employment income. Asset-depletion programs turn eligible assets into a qualifying-income calculation under the lender's rules.

Reviewed August 20, 2026 · Anatoliy Kanevsky · NMLS #2775380 · CA Broker DRE #01385024

Quick answer

Asset depletion is not a single standardized loan. Different lenders decide which assets are eligible, what percentage of each asset can be counted, what must be subtracted for down payment and reserves, and over how many months the remaining assets are converted into qualifying income. The same portfolio can therefore produce different mortgage results at different lenders.

Who should consider an asset-based review

  • Retirees with significant investment or retirement assets but limited earned income.
  • Business owners whose wealth has accumulated in liquid assets while taxable income remains low.
  • Executives or professionals between positions.
  • High-net-worth buyers who prefer not to restructure investments solely to create conventional income.
  • Borrowers receiving irregular distributions, trust income or investment proceeds.

What assets may matter

Depending on the lender and program, the review may consider cash, money-market funds, brokerage assets, stocks, bonds, mutual funds, retirement accounts and other eligible liquid or semi-liquid assets. Not every asset is counted at 100%, and accessibility, ownership, age restrictions and withdrawal penalties can affect the calculation.

The calculation is where lender choice matters

A typical asset-depletion concept looks like this: start with eligible assets, apply any program-specific haircut, subtract funds required for the transaction and reserves, then divide the remaining eligible amount by the lender's required depletion period. That result becomes a calculated monthly income figure for underwriting.

Because each input can vary by lender, it is a mistake to assume one online formula represents the entire market.

What we compare before choosing the program

  • Which assets are eligible and at what percentage.
  • Whether retirement assets can be used and under what conditions.
  • How much must remain after down payment, closing costs and reserves.
  • The lender's depletion period and resulting monthly qualifying income.
  • Credit, occupancy, property type and target loan amount.
  • Asset depletion versus conventional, jumbo, bank-statement or other Non-QM structures.

Asset depletion vs. bank statements

Bank-statement qualifying looks at recurring deposits and business cash flow. Asset depletion looks primarily at accumulated eligible wealth. A borrower with strong ongoing business deposits may fit bank statements better; a borrower with large liquid holdings but inconsistent monthly deposits may fit asset depletion better.

Asset depletion vs. jumbo full-doc

For a high-value California property, the borrower should compare the asset-based option with a conventional or jumbo full-documentation loan whenever ordinary income can support the file. Alternative documentation can solve a qualifying problem, but it is not automatically the least-expensive structure.

What to prepare for a first review

  • Approximate purchase price or property value and loan amount.
  • Occupancy: primary, second home or investment.
  • Approximate credit range.
  • Down payment or equity available.
  • Current cash, brokerage and retirement balances.
  • Whether any assets are pledged, restricted or needed for another purpose.
  • Current income sources, even if they are irregular.
  • Desired amount of liquidity to keep after closing.
