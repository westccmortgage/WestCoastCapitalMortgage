Rebuilding in Pacific Palisades? Solve the Financing Gap Before the Build Starts.

A rebuild can involve insurance proceeds, an existing mortgage, SBA disaster funds, cash reserves and a new construction or mortgage loan at the same time. The first job is to make those pieces work together before the construction budget and draw schedule are locked in.

Anatoliy Kanevsky · NMLS #2775380 · CA Broker DRE #01385024

The rebuild market has moved from claims to construction

Pacific Palisades now has a substantial pipeline of permitted rebuilds and active construction. That changes the mortgage question from “when can I rebuild?” to “how do I close the gap between insurance, existing debt and the actual construction budget?”

For many homeowners, the correct answer is not a single loan product. It is a financing stack that must be reviewed as one plan.

Start with the complete rebuild financing stack

Before comparing rates, map every source of funds and every obligation tied to the property:

  • Insurance proceeds already received and amounts still expected.
  • The current mortgage balance, payment status and any servicer-controlled insurance draws.
  • SBA disaster-loan approval, amount, draw status and remaining availability.
  • Cash and liquid assets you are prepared to contribute.
  • The architect, permit, contractor and hard/soft-cost budget.
  • The remaining funding gap that must be solved with construction or mortgage financing.
  • The intended permanent-loan or refinance strategy after completion.

New in 2026: California's Disaster Rebuilding Assistance Program

California funded the Disaster Rebuilding Assistance Program (DRAP) with $100 million. CalHFA describes lender-side support that can include construction-loan interest-rate buydowns and a loan-loss guarantee intended to help eligible disaster-impacted homeowners access rebuilding financing.

Lender enrollment opened August 3, 2026. That is important, but it does not mean every homeowner can already obtain a DRAP-supported loan from every mortgage company. Participating-lender availability, borrower eligibility and program implementation have to be confirmed at the time of application.

New in August: Lendistry announced a Pacific Palisades and Eaton-fire mortgage pilot

On August 11, 2026, Lendistry Home Loans announced a $20 million Disaster Recovery Mortgage Pilot supported by Bank of America and the California Community Foundation. The announcement said applications were expected in early fall 2026 and described more flexible liquidity and cash-flow treatment for eligible wildfire survivors.

This is a separate program, not a West Coast Capital Mortgage program, and availability is subject to Lendistry's own launch timing, eligibility and underwriting requirements. It is still useful to include it when comparing the homeowner's full rebuild funding plan.

SBA disaster-loan borrowers may have more time to draw approved funds

On July 1, 2026, the U.S. Small Business Administration announced a final extension for January 2025 Los Angeles wildfire survivors. For approved borrowers, the agency said disaster-loan funds can be drawn for up to 24 months from the date of loan authorization.

If you already have an SBA approval, do not treat that approval as a stand-alone number. The mortgage or construction lender should understand the SBA balance, lien position, remaining draw availability and how those funds fit into the project budget.

What a rebuild lender needs to understand

A useful financing review should not begin with a generic prequalification. It should begin with the property and the build:

  • Property address and current land/improvement condition.
  • Insurance declaration page, settlement or scope, proceeds paid and proceeds remaining.
  • Existing mortgage statement and servicer requirements for insurance draws.
  • SBA approval or loan documents, if applicable.
  • Approved plans, permit status and expected construction start.
  • Detailed contractor budget, contingency and draw schedule.
  • Borrower income, assets, credit and current housing obligations.
  • Expected completed value and intended occupancy.

Before signing the final construction contract

Confirm that the financing plan works with the contractor's payment schedule. A lender may require inspections, controlled draws, lien waivers, contingency reserves and specific contractor documentation. A contract that assumes large front-loaded payments can create a problem even when the total project budget looks reasonable.

Also confirm the permanent financing strategy. Some homeowners may use a construction-to-permanent structure, while others may complete the rebuild with one capital stack and refinance afterward. The right path depends on the existing mortgage, equity, income, insurance proceeds and available lender programs.
