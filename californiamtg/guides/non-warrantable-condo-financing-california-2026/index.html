California Condo Loan Denied? The Project Review Rules Changed on August 3.

A buyer can be fully qualified and still lose the loan because the condominium project does not meet the selected program's requirements. The August 3, 2026 agency changes make early project review even more important.

Updated August 21, 2026 · Anatoliy Kanevsky · NMLS #2775380 · CA Broker DRE #01385024

The August 3 change in plain English

For loan applications dated on or after August 3, 2026, Fannie Mae retired the Limited Review process. Established projects that previously could use Limited Review generally must now go through Full Review or, when eligible, a Waiver of Project Review. Freddie Mac's Streamlined Review is likewise limited to applications received before August 3, 2026.

That does not mean every condo suddenly became difficult to finance. It does mean that a building that worked under an older review path may need a different level of project analysis for a new application today.

Why a condo loan can fail even when the borrower qualifies

With a condominium, the lender underwrites two things: the borrower and the project. The borrower can have strong credit, verified income and sufficient assets while the HOA or project creates a separate eligibility problem.

Project review can involve financial condition, reserves, insurance, physical condition, repairs, litigation, commercial space, ownership concentration and other characteristics defined by the applicable program.

Common project issues that can stop a conventional condo loan

  • Material litigation or pre-litigation involving the HOA or project.
  • Critical repairs, structural concerns or significant deferred maintenance.
  • Insurance coverage that does not meet the selected program's requirements.
  • Reserve funding, budget or other project financial issues.
  • Commercial or non-residential space outside the applicable limits.
  • Concentrated ownership by a single individual or entity.
  • Project structure, use or documentation that falls outside agency eligibility.

Do not accept “non-warrantable” as the final diagnosis

“Non-warrantable” is often used as a catch-all label. It is not enough information to choose the next lender. A litigation problem requires a different strategy from an insurance problem. A reserve issue is different from a single-entity ownership issue. The first step is to identify the exact project condition that caused the denial.

If your condo loan was denied, request these documents now

  • The lender's exact project denial reason or project-review findings.
  • The completed condo or HOA questionnaire, if one was obtained.
  • Current master insurance information.
  • HOA budget and financial statements, if available.
  • Reserve study and structural or repair documentation, when relevant.
  • Litigation or legal disclosures, if that was the issue.
  • Special-assessment information.
  • Your purchase contract and closing deadline if you are already in escrow.

What a second review should determine

A useful second look is not simply “try another lender.” It should answer four questions: what exactly failed, whether that issue is an agency rule or a lender overlay, whether another conventional path remains available, and whether a portfolio or alternative program is appropriate for the property and borrower.

Possible financing paths after a project denial

The correct path depends on occupancy, loan amount, credit, down payment, project problem and lender appetite. Depending on the scenario, a mortgage broker may compare:

  • Another conventional lender if the first denial involved an overlay, incomplete review or a project status that can be resolved.
  • A portfolio lender with different condominium project standards.
  • A Non-QM condo program for an eligible owner-occupied, second-home or investment scenario.
  • A DSCR path for an eligible business-purpose investment property.
  • A different leverage structure when additional equity changes the available program set.

Why buyers and agents should review the project before escrow gets tight

If a building has known litigation, insurance, reserve, repair or ownership-concentration concerns, project review should start as early as practical. After the August 3 changes, relying on the fact that another unit obtained conventional financing several years ago is even less useful. The current application date, current project documents and current program rules matter.
